Exploring Multidimensional Aspects of Hope Speech Computationally

A psycholinguistic and emotional perspective: we extract LIWC, NRC-emotion and VADER-sentiment features from tweets, show how different types of hope carry distinct emotional signatures, and use those features to classify hope with gradient-boosting models.

Muhammad Arif, Moein Shahiki Tash, Ainaz Jamshidi, Iqra Ameer, Fida Ullah, Jugal Kalita, Alexander Gelbukh, Fazlourrahman Balouchzahi

The task

Not all hope is the same

Following the PolyHope definition, hope is "an expectation, wish, or desire for something to happen or to be true" — and it can come wrapped in anger or sadness as easily as in joy. Tweets are first classified as Hope vs. Not Hope, then hopeful tweets are split into three fine-grained types.

4,081 tweets

Not Hope

No expressed hope, desire or expectation for the future.

"It's also common sense. You don't need to pray or be religious for that."
2,335 tweets

Generalized Hope

Broad, overarching optimism not tied to a specific event or outcome.

"I hope everyone injured makes a speedy recovery…"
982 tweets

Realistic Hope

Anticipating outcomes that are sensible, significant and achievable.

"You have been working all your life — definitely you can buy a car soon."
858 tweets

Unrealistic Hope

Wishes that defy rational expectation, often driven by anger, sadness or despair.

"I wish I had a tail as big as a kite, just like Libra!"

Methodology

From tweets to emotional fingerprints

Two phases: extract psycholinguistic, emotional and sentiment features and analyse how they relate to each hope type; then feed them to gradient-boosting classifiers, alone and combined with n-grams.

PreprocessEmoji → text, expand abbreviations, drop punctuation & stopwords
ExtractLIWC + NRC emotions + VADER sentiment, plus char/word n-grams
SelectSelectFromModel (random forest) on n-grams
TuneBayesian optimization of hyperparameters
Evaluate5-fold CV · weighted & macro P/R/F1
Psycholinguistic
LIWC-22

Function words, drives, cognition, affect, social processes, motives, perception and more.

Emotions
NRC Emotion Lexicon

Anger, anticipation, disgust, fear, joy, sadness, surprise and trust.

Sentiment
VADER

Positive, negative and neutral scores from a polarity- and intensity-weighted lexicon.

Classifiers: LightGBM, CatBoost and XGBoost, each run with three feature sets — n-grams only; LIWC + emotions + sentiments; and all features combined — under default and tuned hyperparameters.

Results

Emotional features pay off

Adding psycholinguistic, emotional and sentiment features beats n-grams alone for every model, and LightGBM with all features combined is best in both tasks. Averages over 5-fold cross-validation.

Key findings

Three signatures of hope

Generalized Hope

  • Highest joy, anticipation, trust and positive sentiment
  • Most affect-laden language
  • Less focused on practical, everyday matters

Realistic Hope

  • Grounded in what is feasible; highest "drives" score
  • Least conversational — a reserved, pragmatic tone
  • Tied to work, home, school and cultural/political topics

Unrealistic Hope

  • Highest anger, sadness and negative sentiment
  • Fewer words but more cognitive engagement
  • More frequent in discussions of health
